Embedded triangulated surfaces on point sets in ℝ³

Fewer than n! surfaces on n points, for every n ≤ 8

A complete enumeration: every order type of 4, 5, 6, 7 and 8 points in general position in space, and for each one the number of embedded triangulated surfaces that use all of its points. The largest count found is 27,640, on a set of 8 points; 8! is 40,320. Explicit sets exceeding n! for n = 9 to 13 are also given; the 8‑point case, with its 159,433 order types, takes up most of the page.

The problem

Take a set P of n points in ℝ³ in general position, meaning no four of them lie in a plane. A triangulated surface on P is a collection of triangles with corners in P such that every point of P is a corner of some triangle, every edge belongs to exactly two triangles, and the triangles around each point form a single cycle; this makes it a closed orientable surface, and its genus g is read off from Euler's formula V − E + F = 2 − 2g. The surface is embedded if no two of its triangles cross, which for points in general position means that no edge passes through the interior of a triangle. The simplest example is the boundary of a tetrahedron on 4 points; on more points, surfaces with the same vertex set can be bent inwards and outwards at interior points in many ways.

Write S(P) for the number of embedded triangulated spheres on P, and T(P) for the number of embedded triangulated surfaces of any genus. Both depend only on the order type of P, that is, on which side of the plane through each three points each fourth point lies, because "edge crosses triangle" is a condition on those signs alone. So the maximum of S(P) over all n‑point sets can be found by listing the finitely many order types of n points and counting on one representative of each.

The question this page answers is whether any set of at most 8 points has n! or more surfaces. It does not: the maximum of S and of T grows by a factor of roughly two with each added point and reaches 0.69 · 8! at n = 8, and explicit sets with more than n! spheres are known for n = 9 through 13 (listed below), so n = 9 is the first n at which n! is exceeded.

Result for every n ≤ 8

"OM classes" is the number of oriented matroids (order types up to reorientation) that the list falls into, compared with the catalogue of Finschi and Fukuda, a complete, independently generated census. That agreement, together with each list being closed under reorientation, is what proves the lists complete (see Method). For n ≤ 6 no surface other than a sphere fits (a torus needs 12 triangles, but 6 points have only 15 edges, hence at most 10 triangles); for n = 7 and 8 only spheres and tori occur.

What decides the count: how many points are inside the hull

Group the order types by the number of convex‑hull vertices. The averages fall by roughly a third with each additional hull vertex, and the ranges overlap only at their edges: the best sets with five hull vertices beat the weakest sets with a tetrahedral hull, but no set with a given hull size comes close to the best set with one fewer. Interior points are what make spheres plentiful, because an interior point can be dented into a face from inside or bumped out from outside.

Method

Two point sets have the same order type when the orientation (sign of the volume) of every 4‑subset agrees after relabeling, allowing a mirror image. The sphere count depends only on this data, because "edge crosses triangle" is a sign condition. Order types were generated by sampling random integer point sets and then repeatedly moving one point across the nearest plane spanned by three others (one sign flip at a time), from several representatives of every class, until no new class appeared. For the final pass every wall of each stored representative's cell was crossed exactly (the cell of a moving point is a convex polytope cut out by the planes through three other points; its facets are computed and each one is stepped across through its centroid). The same procedure reproduces the known counts of planar order types (3, 16, 135, 3315, 158 817 for 5–9 points) and gives 2, 4, 246 types for 5, 6, 7 points in space. Two independent runs of the program (different random seeds, no shared data) both ended with exactly these 159,433 types, and the completeness argument below makes the list provably complete. With 8 points a surface has at most 18 triangles (each of the 28 edges lies in two of them), so only spheres (12 triangles) and tori (16) can occur; genus 2 would need 20. Surfaces were counted by direct backtracking over oriented triangles: each oriented edge used once, triangles never pierced by edges, every vertex link a single loop; genus from V − F/2 = 2 − 2g.

Completeness. A point configuration is an acyclic reorientation of its oriented matroid, and reorienting a realizable oriented matroid keeps it realizable. For each list above, all 2ⁿ reorientations of every entry were generated, the acyclic ones canonicalized, and the result compared with the list: nothing outside the list appeared (the list is closed under reorientation), and the number of oriented‑matroid classes equals the published number of realizable uniform rank‑4 oriented matroids on n elements (1, 1, 1, 11, 2,604 for n = 4…8: the catalogue of Finschi and Fukuda gives 1, 1, 1, 11, 2,628 uniform oriented matroids, and Fukuda, Miyata and Moriyama, Complete enumeration of small realizable oriented matroids, Discrete & Computational Geometry 49 (2013), Table 2, show that exactly 2,604 of the 2,628 are realizable). An order type missing from the list would lie in one of those classes, and the closure of that class is in the list, so none is missing. The 7‑point list was also compared entry by entry with the catalogue's 5,083 abstract order types on 7 elements of rank 4: its 246 non‑degenerate ones are exactly ours.
